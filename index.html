Start Here: Conducting the Visit

Core approach: use family-centered, nonstigmatizing care; evaluate comorbidities and contextual drivers; provide or refer for intensive health behavior and lifestyle treatment; and add pharmacotherapy or surgical evaluation when indicated.

New patient

Work through Steps 1–9 in order. Document growth trajectory, disease burden, family priorities, and your treatment plan.

Follow-up

Jump to Step 10, then revisit any domain that has changed: medication, labs, sleep, mental health, nutrition, activity, genetics, or surgery eligibility.

Scope: this playbook is an educational clinical reference, not an institutional protocol or formal endorsement by OMA, AAP, FDA, NASPGHAN, ASMBS, ABOM, or any institution.

1. Classify Growth and Severity

Measure and plot height and weight. Calculate age- and sex-specific BMI percentile for children age 2 years and older.

CategoryDefinition
OverweightBMI ≥85th to <95th percentile
ObesityBMI ≥95th percentile
Severe obesityBMI ≥120% of the 95th percentile

Growth clues that change the differential

  • Review height velocity and the longitudinal growth curve, not a single BMI value.
  • Poor linear growth or height deceleration with increasing adiposity should raise concern for a secondary/pathologic process.
  • Severe obesity beginning early in childhood, especially before age 5 with marked hyperphagia, should prompt consideration of genetic or syndromic obesity.
Severe obesity: %BMIp95 can help characterize longitudinal change when BMI is well above the 95th percentile.

2. History: Ask What Changes Management

Weight trajectory

  • Age of onset
  • Rapid gain or early severe obesity
  • Prior treatment and response

Eating and hunger

  • Meal pattern and beverages
  • Portions and second servings
  • Hyperphagia/food seeking
  • Loss-of-control or binge-type eating
  • Food insecurity

Activity and function

  • Enjoyed activities
  • Endurance
  • Pain or limp
  • Prior injuries
  • Sedentary time

Sleep

  • Duration and routine
  • Snoring
  • Witnessed apnea
  • Daytime impairment

Medication review

  • Weight-promoting medications
  • Psychiatric medications
  • Steroid exposure
  • Current obesity treatment

Psychosocial

  • Weight-based teasing/stigma
  • Depression/anxiety
  • Body image
  • School avoidance
  • Family stressors and barriers
Useful opener: “What changes would you most like to see in your child’s health?”

4. Screening and Laboratory Evaluation

Do not order the same panel for every child. Screening should reflect age, BMI category, risk factors, prior results, symptoms, and guideline recommendations.
Clinical situationWhat to consider
Age 10+ with obesityEvaluate for lipid abnormalities, abnormal glucose metabolism, and abnormal liver function.
Age 10+ with overweightEvaluate lipids; consider glucose/liver evaluation when additional risk factors are present.
Age 2–9 with obesityLipid evaluation may be considered.
Liver risk / obesityNASPGHAN supports ALT screening around ages 9–11, earlier in selected higher-risk patients.

Liver pearl

  • Pediatric ALT upper limits used in NASPGHAN guidance: 22 U/L for girls and 26 U/L for boys.
  • ALT >2× ULN persisting for >3 months warrants evaluation for MASLD and other chronic hepatitis causes.
  • Routine ultrasound is not recommended as the screening test.

5. Review Obesity-Related Comorbidities

Cardiometabolic

Hypertension, dyslipidemia, abnormal glucose metabolism, type 2 diabetes risk.

Hepatic

MASLD or other causes of persistent ALT elevation.

Sleep

Habitual snoring, witnessed apnea, daytime impairment. Polysomnography is indicated when obesity is accompanied by symptoms of disordered breathing.

Orthopedic

Hip/groin/knee pain, limp, activity limitation. Think SCFE when clinically concerning.

Neurologic

Headache with papilledema or visual symptoms. Think idiopathic intracranial hypertension.

Mental health

Depression, anxiety, weight stigma, disordered eating, loss-of-control eating, acute safety concerns.

6. Genetic and Syndromic Obesity

Consider a genetic or syndromic contribution when the phenotype includes:

  • Severe obesity beginning early in childhood, particularly before age 5.
  • Marked hyperphagia or food-seeking behavior.
  • Developmental delay, hypotonia, dysmorphic features, endocrine abnormalities, retinal disease, polydactyly, renal abnormalities, or other syndromic findings.
  • A phenotype suggestive of Prader-Willi syndrome, Bardet-Biedl syndrome, hypothalamic obesity, or a monogenic melanocortin-pathway disorder.
Next step: consider pediatric endocrinology, genetics, or specialized obesity-program referral for phenotype-directed evaluation/testing rather than automatically ordering the same genetic panel for every child.

Setmelanotide

Targeted therapy for specific melanocortin-pathway disorders. Current labeling includes qualifying patients age 2 years and older with POMC, PCSK1 or LEPR deficiency and Bardet-Biedl syndrome. Eligibility and diagnostic confirmation must follow the current label.

7. Foundational Treatment

Principle: pharmacotherapy and surgery are adjuncts to longitudinal health behavior and lifestyle treatment, not replacements for it.

Nutrition

Assess age-appropriate nutritional adequacy, meal pattern, beverages, food access, dietary quality, protein-containing foods, and micronutrient risk. Avoid prescribing a universal high-protein target solely because a child is using a GLP-1 medication.

Activity

Use enjoyable, developmentally appropriate movement. Account for pain, orthopedic disease, conditioning, confidence, and access.

Sleep

Address duration, routine, snoring and apnea symptoms. Treat obesity and sleep-disordered breathing concurrently.

Behavioral health

Use family-centered, nonstigmatizing care. Address teasing, depression/anxiety, disordered eating, and barriers that make the plan unrealistic.

8. Pharmacotherapy

For adolescents age 12 years and older with obesity, AAP guidance recommends offering weight-loss pharmacotherapy according to medication-specific indications, risks, and benefits as an adjunct to health behavior and lifestyle treatment.

GLP-1 practical checklist

  • Confirm the patient meets the specific pediatric indication for the selected product.
  • Review injection readiness, access, concurrent medications, pregnancy considerations when relevant, MTC/MEN2 history, GI history, gallbladder/pancreatitis history, and dehydration/renal vulnerability.
  • For common GI symptoms, use smaller portions, slower eating, hydration, and identify foods that worsen symptoms.
  • Persistent severe abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, inability to maintain hydration, or symptoms concerning for pancreatitis/gallbladder disease require clinical evaluation.
  • Use product-specific response/discontinuation rules. Do not impose one universal percentage-loss stopping rule across pediatric AOMs.

9. Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery Referral

AAP guidance recommends offering referral for adolescents age 13 years and older with severe obesity for evaluation at a comprehensive multidisciplinary pediatric metabolic and bariatric surgery center.

Referral is evaluation, not commitment to surgery.
SeverityReferral framework
Class II severe obesityBMI ≥120% of the 95th percentile or BMI ≥35 kg/m², with clinically significant obesity-related disease, using the receiving program's exact criteria.
Class III severe obesityBMI ≥140% of the 95th percentile or BMI ≥40 kg/m², generally without requiring a comorbidity.
  • Vertical sleeve gastrectomy and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass are established adolescent procedures; selection is individualized.
  • Discuss expected benefits and risks probabilistically.
  • Long-term nutritional, psychosocial and procedure-specific surveillance is essential.

10. Follow-Up Visit

Growth

Weight, height, BMI percentile, %BMIp95 when useful, height velocity, pubertal context.

Comorbidities

BP, sleep/snoring, headaches/visual symptoms, musculoskeletal symptoms, menstrual/PCOS symptoms, and indicated labs.

Lifestyle/context

Family-selected goals, dietary pattern, beverage intake, activity, sedentary time, sleep, food security and barriers.

Medication

Indication, access, adherence, actual dose, titration, adverse effects, precautions, mood, BP/HR when relevant.

Mental health

Weight stigma, depression/anxiety, disordered eating, loss-of-control eating, body image and safety.

Next step

Continue or intensify treatment, adjust AOM if appropriate, coordinate nutrition/behavioral health, make subspecialty referrals, consider MBS evaluation when indicated.

One-Page Quick Reference

CLASSIFY: overweight ≥85th to <95th percentile; obesity ≥95th; severe obesity ≥120% of the 95th percentile.
SCREEN: age 10+ with obesity: evaluate lipids, abnormal glucose metabolism and abnormal liver function. Age 2–9 with obesity: lipid evaluation may be considered.
PHARMACOTHERAPY: offer adolescents age 12+ with obesity pharmacotherapy according to medication-specific indications, risks and benefits, as an adjunct to health behavior/lifestyle treatment.
MBS: offer referral for adolescents age 13+ with severe obesity for evaluation at a comprehensive multidisciplinary pediatric MBS center.
RED FLAGS: limp/hip pain → SCFE pathway; papilledema/visual symptoms → urgent IIH pathway; severe early-onset obesity + hyperphagia → genetic/syndromic evaluation; poor linear growth → consider secondary cause; acute psychiatric safety concern → immediate safety pathway.
